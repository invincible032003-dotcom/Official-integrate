@topic Weighted Aggregative Formulae
Q: The Laspeyres price index is given by:
A) Σp1q0 / Σp0q0 × 100
B) Σp1q1 / Σp0q1 × 100
C) Σp1(q0 + q1) / Σp0(q0 + q1) × 100
D) √(Σp1q0 / Σp0q0 × Σp1q1 / Σp0q1) × 100
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: **Laspeyres** weights prices by **base-period quantities (q0)**. **Paasche** uses **current quantities (q1)**. **Marshall-Edgeworth** uses **(q0 + q1)**. **Fisher** is the **geometric mean of L and P**.
SC: Laspeyres uses base weights (q0); Paasche uses current weights (q1).

Q: The Paasche price index uses as weights:
A) current-period quantities
B) base-period quantities
C) the average of base and current quantities
D) fixed quantities of an arbitrary period
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: **Paasche = Σp1q1 / Σp0q1 × 100**, weighted by **current quantities**. A **fixed arbitrary period's quantities** give **Kelly's** fixed-weight index.
SC: Paasche uses present-period quantities.

Q: Fisher's ideal price index is:
A) the geometric mean of the Laspeyres and Paasche indices
B) the arithmetic mean of the Laspeyres and Paasche indices
C) the harmonic mean of the Laspeyres and Paasche indices
D) the ratio of the Laspeyres to the Paasche index
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: **Fisher = √(L × P)**. The **arithmetic mean (L + P)/2** is the **Dorbish-Bowley** index.
SC: Fisher uses the GM; Bowley uses the AM.

Q: Match List-I (Index) with List-II (Weights / construction):
| List-I | List-II |
|---|---|
| A. Marshall-Edgeworth | 1. √(q0 q1) |
| B. Walsh | 2. (q0 + q1) |
| C. Kelly | 3. (L + P) / 2 |
| D. Dorbish-Bowley | 4. Fixed quantities of a chosen period |
A) A-2, B-1, C-4, D-3
B) A-1, B-2, C-4, D-3
C) A-2, B-1, C-3, D-4
D) A-4, B-1, C-2, D-3
ANS: A
T: Match
D: Medium
EXP: The weights are **Marshall-Edgeworth: q0 + q1**; **Walsh: √(q0q1)**; **Kelly: fixed quantities of any chosen period**. **Dorbish-Bowley** is the **arithmetic mean of L and P**.
SC: ME adds the quantities, Walsh takes their root, Kelly fixes them, Bowley averages L and P.

Q: The Laspeyres quantity index is:
A) Σq1p0 / Σq0p0 × 100
B) Σq1p1 / Σq0p1 × 100
C) Σq1p1 / Σq0p0 × 100
D) Σq0p1 / Σq1p0 × 100
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: A **quantity index** swaps the roles of p and q. **Laspeyres quantity index** = Σq1p0/Σq0p0, weighted by **base prices**. The **IIP** is a **Laspeyres-type quantity index**.
SC: For a quantity index, swap p and q; Laspeyres still uses base-period weights.

Q: The value index V01 is defined as:
A) Σp1q1 / Σp0q0 × 100
B) Σp1q0 / Σp0q0 × 100
C) Σp1q1 / Σp0q1 × 100
D) Σq1p0 / Σq0p0 × 100
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: The **value index** compares total values: **Σp1q1/Σp0q0**. It changes because **both prices and quantities** change.
SC: Value index = current value ÷ base value.

@topic Tests of Adequacy
Q: The time reversal test requires that:
A) P01 × P10 = 1
B) P01 × Q01 = V01
C) P01 × P12 × P20 = 1
D) P01 = Q01
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: **Time reversal**: an index computed forward, times the index computed backward, equals **1**. **Factor reversal**: **P01 × Q01 = V01**. **Circular test**: **P01 × P12 × P20 = 1**.
SC: Time reversal: forward × backward = 1.

Q: Which of the following index numbers satisfies both the time reversal test and the factor reversal test?
A) Fisher's ideal index
B) Laspeyres' index
C) Paasche's index
D) Marshall-Edgeworth index
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: **Fisher's index** satisfies **both time and factor reversal**, hence the name "**ideal**". **Marshall-Edgeworth** satisfies **time reversal only**. **Laspeyres and Paasche** satisfy **neither**. Fisher does **not** satisfy the **circular test**.
SC: Fisher passes both reversal tests but fails the circular test.

Q: Which of the following satisfies the circular test?
A) Kelly's fixed-weight aggregative index
B) Fisher's ideal index
C) Laspeyres' index
D) Paasche's index
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: The **circular test** is satisfied by **Kelly's fixed-weight aggregative index** and by the **simple geometric mean of price relatives** (and the simple aggregative index). **Fisher fails it**, as do Laspeyres and Paasche.
SC: Circular test: only fixed weights (Kelly) or a simple GM pass it.

Q: Consider the following statements:
1. Marshall-Edgeworth index satisfies the time reversal test.
2. Laspeyres' index satisfies the factor reversal test.
3. Dorbish-Bowley index satisfies the time reversal test.
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?
A) 1 and 3 only
B) 1 only
C) 2 and 3 only
D) 1, 2 and 3
ANS: B
T: Statement
D: Medium
EXP: **Marshall-Edgeworth passes time reversal**. **Laspeyres fails factor reversal** (and time reversal). **Dorbish-Bowley (the AM of L and P) fails time reversal**. Fisher (the GM of L and P) passes it.
SC: The AM of L and P fails time reversal; the GM (Fisher) passes it.

Q: The simple aggregative price index Σp1/Σp0 fails which test?
A) Unit test
B) Time reversal test
C) Circular test
D) It fails none of the tests
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: The **simple aggregative index** depends on the **units of measurement** (per kg vs per quintal), so it **fails the unit test**. It does satisfy **time reversal and circular** tests. All weighted formulae satisfy the unit test.
SC: Adding raw prices is unit-dependent, so the unit test fails.

Q: The factor reversal test is satisfied if:
A) the price index multiplied by the corresponding quantity index equals the value index
B) the price index equals the quantity index
C) the product of forward and backward indices equals one
D) the index is independent of units
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: **Factor reversal**: interchanging p and q gives a quantity index Q01 such that **P01 × Q01 = Σp1q1/Σp0q0**. Only **Fisher** passes it among the common formulae.
SC: Price index × quantity index = value index.

Q: Laspeyres price index × Paasche quantity index equals:
A) the value index
B) Fisher's price index
C) 1
D) the Laspeyres quantity index
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Hard
EXP: **L_p × P_q = (Σp1q0/Σp0q0) × (Σq1p1/Σq0p1) = Σp1q1/Σp0q0 = V**. Likewise **P_p × L_q = V**. This "crossed" pair satisfies factor reversal, although neither index does alone.
SC: A crossed Laspeyres-Paasche pair multiplies to the value index.

@topic Weighted Averages of Relatives
Q: The weighted arithmetic mean of price relatives (p1/p0), with base-period values (p0q0) as weights, gives:
A) Laspeyres' price index
B) Paasche's price index
C) Fisher's index
D) Kelly's index
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: **Σ(p1/p0)·p0q0 / Σp0q0 = Σp1q0/Σp0q0 = Laspeyres**. This is how CPIs are built in practice, as an expenditure-share-weighted average of price relatives.
SC: AM of relatives with base-value weights = Laspeyres.

Q: The weighted harmonic mean of price relatives, with current-period values (p1q1) as weights, gives:
A) Paasche's price index
B) Laspeyres' price index
C) Marshall-Edgeworth index
D) Fisher's index
ANS: A
D: Hard
EXP: **HM = Σp1q1 / Σ[(p0/p1)·p1q1] = Σp1q1/Σp0q1 = Paasche**. The weighted AM of relatives with weights p0q1 also gives Paasche.
SC: HM of relatives with current-value weights = Paasche.

Q: The family budget method of constructing a cost of living index is equivalent to:
A) Laspeyres' index, using the weighted arithmetic mean of price relatives with base-period expenditure weights
B) Paasche's index
C) Fisher's index
D) a simple geometric mean of prices
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: The **family budget method** takes **I = ΣIW/ΣW**, where **W = p0q0** (base expenditure). That equals **Laspeyres**. The **aggregate expenditure method** is **Σp1q0/Σp0q0**, which is also Laspeyres.
SC: Both cost-of-living methods reduce to Laspeyres.

Q: At the elementary (item) level, India's CPI (base 2012) aggregates price relatives of quotations using the:
A) geometric mean (Jevons type)
B) arithmetic mean
C) harmonic mean
D) median
ANS: A
D: Hard
EXP: The **CPI (2012)** uses the **geometric mean of price relatives** at the **elementary level**, as recommended internationally (Jevons). **Laspeyres-type** weighting is used above that level.
SC: Elementary level uses the GM; higher levels use Laspeyres weights.

@topic Bias & Relationships
Q: When prices and quantities are negatively correlated (consumers shift away from items whose prices rise), generally:
A) Laspeyres index > Fisher index > Paasche index
B) Paasche index > Fisher index > Laspeyres index
C) Laspeyres index = Paasche index
D) Fisher index > Laspeyres index > Paasche index
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: With substitution away from dearer goods, **base weights overstate** and **current weights understate** the price rise. So **L > P**, and **Fisher (their GM) lies between them**.
SC: Laspeyres is biased upward, Paasche downward, and Fisher sits in between.

Q: Assertion (A): Laspeyres' price index tends to overstate the rise in the cost of living.
Reason (R): It ignores the substitution of relatively cheaper goods by consumers, as it uses fixed base-period quantities.
A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
C) A is true, but R is false
D) A is false, but R is true
ANS: A
T: Assertion-Reason
D: Medium
EXP: Fixed **base quantities** ignore **substitution**, so the index gives too much weight to goods whose prices rose. That is the **upward (substitution) bias**. R explains A.
SC: Fixed weights ignore substitution, which biases Laspeyres upward.

Q: The Laspeyres and Paasche price indices will be equal when:
A) all prices change in the same proportion, or all quantities change in the same proportion
B) the base year is a normal year
C) the number of items is large
D) the index is computed on a chain base
ANS: A
D: Hard
EXP: If **all price relatives are equal**, or **all quantities change proportionally** (identical relative weights), then **L = P**, and Fisher equals both.
SC: L = P when prices or quantities move proportionally.

Q: The GDP deflator is best described as:
A) an implicit, Paasche-type price index = nominal GDP ÷ real GDP × 100
B) a Laspeyres index with fixed base weights
C) a consumer price index for urban areas
D) a wholesale price index for services
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: **GDP deflator = nominal / real × 100**. It uses **current-period quantities**, so it is **Paasche-type** and implicit. It covers **all goods and services** in GDP, unlike CPI or WPI.
SC: The deflator is current-weighted (Paasche) and covers the whole economy.

@topic Numericals
Q: Base year: prices (2, 5), quantities (10, 4). Current year: prices (3, 6), quantities (8, 5). The Laspeyres price index is:
A) 135
B) 131.7
C) 133.3
D) 140
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Medium
EXP: **L = Σp1q0/Σp0q0 = (3×10 + 6×4)/(2×10 + 5×4) = 54/40 × 100 = 135**.
SC: Price the base-year basket at today's prices.

Q: For the same data, base prices (2, 5), base quantities (10, 4), current prices (3, 6), current quantities (8, 5), the Paasche price index is approximately:
A) 131.7
B) 135.0
C) 133.3
D) 128.0
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Medium
EXP: **P = Σp1q1/Σp0q1 = (3×8 + 6×5)/(2×8 + 5×5) = 54/41 × 100 ≈ 131.7**.
SC: Price the current basket at both sets of prices.

Q: If the Laspeyres index is 135 and the Paasche index is 131.7, Fisher's ideal index is approximately:
A) 133.3
B) 132.0
C) 131.7
D) 135.0
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Medium
EXP: **Fisher = √(135 × 131.7) = √17779.5 ≈ 133.34**, i.e. **133.3** to one decimal place. The **Bowley** index (AM) would be **133.35**, slightly above the GM.
SC: GM ≤ AM, so Fisher sits just below Bowley.

Q: A price index with base 2015 = 100 stands at 150 in 2018 and 180 in 2022. If the base is shifted to 2018, the index for 2022 becomes:
A) 120
B) 130
C) 180
D) 270
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Easy
EXP: **Base shifting: new index = old index ÷ old index of the new base × 100 = 180/150 × 100 = 120**.
SC: Divide by the new base's old value.

Q: An old series (base 2004-05 = 100) stands at 200 in 2011-12. A new series starts with 2011-12 = 100 and stands at 125 in 2015-16. Spliced to the old base, the 2015-16 index is:
A) 250
B) 225
C) 125
D) 162.5
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Medium
EXP: **Splicing forward: 125 × 200/100 = 250**. The splicing factor is the **old series' value in the link year** (200) divided by 100.
SC: Multiply the new series by the old series' link-year value ÷ 100.

Q: A worker's money wage rises from ₹20,000 to ₹30,000 while the CPI rises from 100 to 160. The worker's real wage:
A) falls by 6.25%
B) rises by 50%
C) rises by 6.25%
D) is unchanged
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Medium
EXP: **Real wage = 30,000 × 100/160 = ₹18,750**, compared with ₹20,000 before. That is a fall of **1,250/20,000 = 6.25%**. This is **deflating** by a price index.
SC: Real value = money value ÷ price index × 100.

Q: If the consumer price index rises from 100 to 125, the purchasing power of a rupee becomes:
A) 80 paise
B) 75 paise
C) ₹1.25
D) 125 paise
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Easy
EXP: **Purchasing power = 100/125 = 0.80**, i.e. **80 paise**. Purchasing power of money is the **reciprocal** of the price index.
SC: Purchasing power = 100 ÷ CPI.

Q: The link relatives for years 2, 3 and 4 (each year compared with the previous one) are 110, 120 and 90. With year 1 = 100, the chain index for year 4 is:
A) 118.8
B) 120.0
C) 108.0
D) 106.7
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Medium
EXP: The chain index is the **cumulative product of link relatives**: **100 × 1.10 × 1.20 × 0.90 = 118.8**. Year 2 = 110 and year 3 = 132.
SC: Multiply the link relatives in succession.

@topic Chain Base & Construction Issues
Q: Which of the following is an advantage of the chain base method of index numbers?
A) New items can be introduced and old items dropped without recomputing the whole series
B) It always equals the fixed base index
C) It is free from cumulative errors
D) It requires no link relatives
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: **Chain base**: each year is compared with the **previous year** (link relatives), then chained. Items and weights can be **updated easily**. The drawback is that **errors cumulate** and there is **chain drift** over long periods.
SC: Chaining is flexible, but its errors cumulate.

Q: Chain base and fixed base index numbers will be the same when:
A) there is only one commodity (or the weights and basket stay unchanged throughout)
B) the base year is normal
C) the number of years is even
D) prices are rising
ANS: A
D: Hard
EXP: For **a single series of relatives**, product of the links = fixed base relative, so both coincide. With **multiple commodities and changing weights** they generally differ.
SC: One item: chain equals fixed; many items: they diverge.

Q: Which of the following is NOT a desirable criterion for choosing the base period of an index number?
A) It should be a year of abnormal price movements, to capture volatility
B) It should be a normal year
C) It should not be too distant from the current period
D) Reliable price and weight data should be available for it
ANS: A
T: Odd-one-out
D: Easy
EXP: The base should be **normal**, **recent**, and **data-rich**, and it should match the **expenditure survey** year. An **abnormal year** is exactly what to avoid, as seen with 2009-10 in the national accounts.
SC: The base year must be normal.

Q: Which of the following are recognised sources of error in index numbers?
1. Formula error
2. Sampling error in selecting items or markets
3. Homogeneity error from changes in quality or the basket over time
Select the correct answer using the code given below:
A) 1 only
B) 1 and 2 only
C) 2 and 3 only
D) 1, 2 and 3
ANS: D
T: Statement
D: Medium
EXP: All three are sources of error: **formula error** (no formula is perfect), **sampling error** (items, markets, quotations) and **homogeneity error** (quality change, new or disappearing items).
SC: Index errors come from the formula, the sample and homogeneity.

Q: Weighted index numbers are generally preferred to unweighted ones because:
A) they reflect the relative importance of items in expenditure or production
B) they are easier to compute
C) they always satisfy the circular test
D) they eliminate sampling error
ANS: A
T: Conceptual
D: Easy
EXP: Weights reflect **importance**, such as expenditure share for CPI, net traded value for WPI and value added for IIP. Unweighted indices treat salt and cars alike.
SC: Weights reflect how much each item matters.

@topic Official & Market Indices
Q: The base period and base value of the BSE Sensex are:
A) 1978-79 = 100
B) 1995 = 1000
C) 2004-05 = 100
D) 1983-84 = 1000
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: **Sensex (30 stocks)** has **base 1978-79 = 100**. **Nifty 50** has **base 3 Nov 1995 = 1000**. Both are **free-float market-capitalisation weighted**.
SC: Sensex base 1978-79 = 100; Nifty base 1995 = 1000.

Q: The Nifty 50 index of the National Stock Exchange has a base value of:
A) 1000
B) 100
C) 500
D) 10
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: **Nifty 50** has a base value of **1000** (base date **3 November 1995**). The **Sensex** has a base value of **100** (1978-79).
SC: Nifty 1000, Sensex 100.

Q: Which of the following Indian indices is a quantity (volume) index rather than a price index?
A) Index of Industrial Production
B) Wholesale Price Index
C) CPI-IW
D) GDP deflator
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: The **IIP** measures **volume of output** (quantity index, Laspeyres). The rest are **price** measures.
SC: IIP measures quantities; the others measure prices.

Q: The Wholesale Price Index measures price changes at:
A) the first point of bulk transaction (ex-factory, ex-mine, mandi)
B) the retail level paid by consumers
C) the import border only
D) the level of services
ANS: A
D: Easy
EXP: **WPI** prices goods at the **first point of bulk sale**. The **CPI** measures **retail** prices, including **services**. WPI excludes services and indirect taxes.
SC: WPI prices the first bulk sale; CPI prices the shop counter.

Q: Assertion (A): India's inflation-targeting framework uses CPI rather than WPI.
Reason (R): CPI reflects retail prices paid by households, including services, while WPI excludes services.
A) Both A and R are true, and R is the correct explanation of A
B) Both A and R are true, but R is not the correct explanation of A
C) A is true, but R is false
D) A is false, but R is true
ANS: A
T: Assertion-Reason
D: Medium
EXP: The RBI (since the 2016 amendment of the **RBI Act**) targets **CPI-Combined** inflation at **4% ± 2%**, because it measures the **cost of living** of households, including **services**. R explains A.
SC: Households feel the CPI, so the RBI targets it.

Q: The circular test is an extension of which test?
A) Time reversal test
B) Factor reversal test
C) Unit test
D) Homogeneity test
ANS: A
D: Medium
EXP: The **circular test** extends **time reversal** to three or more periods: P01 × P12 × P20 = 1. It allows the **base to be shifted** without recomputing the index. Fisher passes time reversal but **fails the circular test**.
SC: Circular = time reversal taken around a loop of periods.

Q: Which of the following index formulae does NOT use quantities of both periods?
A) Laspeyres' index
B) Fisher's index
C) Marshall-Edgeworth index
D) Walsh's index
ANS: A
T: Odd-one-out
D: Easy
EXP: **Laspeyres** needs **q0 only**, which is why it is practical for monthly CPI and WPI. Fisher, Marshall-Edgeworth and Walsh all need **q0 and q1**.
SC: Only Laspeyres needs just base-period quantities.

Q: Why do official price indices like CPI and WPI generally use a Laspeyres-type formula?
A) Current-period quantities are not available every month, while base weights from a benchmark survey are
B) Laspeyres satisfies all tests
C) Laspeyres has no bias
D) International standards prohibit Fisher's index
ANS: A
T: Conceptual
D: Medium
EXP: Monthly indices cannot wait for **current quantities**. Laspeyres needs only **base weights** (from the CES for CPI, net traded value for WPI) plus current prices. It is **practical**, not bias-free.
SC: Laspeyres is chosen for practicality, not perfection.

Q: A price index for 2024 (base 2012 = 100) is 190. The percentage rise in prices between 2012 and 2024 is:
A) 90%
B) 190%
C) 47.4%
D) 19%
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Easy
EXP: Index 190 means prices are **1.9 times** base, a **90% rise**. The index minus 100 gives the percentage change from the base.
SC: Index − 100 = % change from the base.

Q: If the CPI moves from 150 in March 2024 to 162 in March 2025, the point-to-point annual inflation rate is:
A) 8%
B) 12%
C) 7.4%
D) 1.2%
ANS: A
T: Numerical
D: Easy
EXP: **Inflation = (162 − 150)/150 × 100 = 8%**. The rate is based on the **earlier index**, not on index points.
SC: Inflation = change ÷ old index.
